How Does the Outer Space Treaty Affect Space Trash Cleanup?

Imagine if every piece of trash on Earth still legally belonged to the person who threw it away, meaning no one else could collect it without permission. A similar problem exists above Earth: even a broken or abandoned satellite remains the property of the country that registered it. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty created order in space, but decades later, its ownership rules complicate efforts to clean up the growing amount of orbital debris.

The Outer Space Treaty was originally developed to mainly prevent the placement of nuclear weapons in space. During that time period, the United States and the Soviet Union were engaged in a Cold War, and it became quite obvious how dangerous nuclear weapons could be. The United Nations began to insist on an arms-control treaty for outer space, which led to the adoption of the Outer Space Treaty in 1967.

There were many key ideas explored in this treaty that continue to influence how we venture in space today. The main principle of this treaty included not placing any weapon of mass destruction in space and exploring other celestial bodies for matters of peace. Additionally, it was also discussed that all countries could explore in space, as long as it was for beneficial purposes. However, the main rule that makes it more challenging to find solutions for the growing issue of space debris is the following: the state of registry retains complete jurisdiction and control over its space objects, even when they become non-functional junk or broken fragments.

The issue with the Outer Space Treaty is that it is outdated due it being published decades ago, before the problem of space debris became such a major issue. This rule shows that a dead satellite does not automatically become abandoned property, but could still be connected to its original owner or country of origin. This means another country generally can not capture, move, recycle, or destroy a piece of space debris without that specific country’s permission. The permission could include identifying the debris, locating the owner, researching a removal plan, and negotiating who would be liable if something happened that is different from their removal plan. These protections prevent countries from interfering with each other’s spacecraft, but also slow international cleanup efforts.

Moreover, there are many confusions regarding the topic of space debris and how it should be solved in accordance with the Outer Space Treaty. Article IX explains that countries need to consider the interests of other countries. This “due regard” principle could mean that countries should not allow for the formation of space debris, as it poses many hazards to other nations’ spacecraft. Article IX also includes that countries should avoid “harmful contamination”. However, it is still unclear if space debris and abandoned satellites legally count as contamination.

Additionally, Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty states that countries need to be responsible for their space activities. This includes activities performed by private companies. Unfortunately, countries are not taking enough responsibility for the formation of space debris. Furthermore, the treaty does not define what countries should do to prevent debris.

The Outer Space Treaty was created to promote cooperation during the beginning of the Space Age, not to regulate today’s growing debris problem. Although its principles establish national responsibility and protect ownership of space objects, its vague language provides no clear process for removing abandoned satellites or holding debris creators accountable. As Earth’s orbit becomes increasingly crowded, countries need an updated international agreement that establishes prevention standards, permission procedures, and cleanup responsibilities. Without modernization, a treaty intended to protect space exploration may permanently damage the orbital environment.
