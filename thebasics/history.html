A History of Space Trash

On October 4, 1957, the launch of Sputnik 1 began the Space Age. It also unknowingly began another legacy—one that would fill Earth's orbit with millions of pieces of space trash. Nearly seventy years later, its consequences continue to shape humanity's journey beyond Earth.

The launch of Sputnik 1 led to the creation of the first pieces of human-made orbital debris: the rocket stage that launched the satellite and the satellite itself. Soon after this launch, the United States Air Force created Project Space Track: a network that monitored artificial space objects belonging to the United States and other countries. The need to identify objects in orbit that could pose a military threat to the United States led America to establish advanced radar systems. These systems became capable of tracking missiles, satellites, and eventually space trash. This began the era of tracking objects in Earth’s orbit.

In the 1960s, space activity increased exponentially, and the amount of space debris skyrocketed. For example, on June 29, 1961, a Thor-Ablestar rocket upper stage exploded, tripling the number of space debris objects tracked in space by the North American Aerospace Defense Command Space Object Catalog. Since 1961, more than 500 fragmentation events have occurred in orbit, with only 7 being satellite collisions. The main cause of these events was related to fuel or other energy sources leftover from rocket stages. At this point, space trash was merely a moderate inconvenience. This attitude shifted in 1978 when NASA scientists Don Kessler and Burton Cour-Palais introduced the theory of Kessler Syndrome. This theory warned that collisions between orbiting objects could trigger a chain reaction that produces debris faster than it could be removed. This paper transformed space debris from an engineering obstacle to a global problem.

In 1979, NASA established the Orbital Debris Program Office (ODPO), which consulted with the European Space Agency (ESA) about the reentries of radioactive debris from Cosmos 954 in 1977 and Skylab in 1979. In the 1980s, the Air Force Space Debris Research Program was created to investigate the fragmentation events of Delta rocket systems and the aftereffects of the United States participating in antisatellite testing activities. In 1993, the Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee (IADC) was founded to develop guidelines to address space debris and its risks. Furthermore, the Aerospace Corporation developed a breakup modeling code, IMPACT, and a collision hazard assessment tool, DEBRIS, in the late 1980s and early 1990s. The U.S. government approved the Orbital Debris Mitigation Standard Practices (ODMSP) in 2001 to prevent the growth of space debris, something that other countries and IADC began to follow as well.

However, the issue of space debris continued to progress rapidly. In 1986, the breakup of Ariane 1 created about 500 fragments, and 10 years later, the Pegasus/HAPS breakup led to 750 trackable fragments. In 2007, the Chinese FY-1CASAT test added more than 3,500 fragments, which increased the space object population by 25%. In 2009, Iridium 33 and Russian Cosmos 2251 collided at 11.7 km/s, creating more than 2,300 pieces, some of which have even reentered the atmosphere. Additionally, 2,460 solid rocket-motor firings have occurred, releasing space debris in the form of aluminum oxide. Another important source of the growth of space objects was the ejection of reactor cores from retired Russian radar ocean reconnaissance satellites. These events have led to more than 9,000 metric tons of space trash orbiting Earth.

In recent decades, governments have continued to improve space debris monitoring. The Aerospace Corporation created the Debris Analysis Response Team (DART) in response to the Chinese antisatellite test in 2007 and established the Aerospace Debris Environment Projection Tool (ADEPT) to examine the future projections of space debris. Later, the U.S. Space Force’s Space Fence was established as an advanced radar system for tracking space debris.

Today, scientists estimate that about 128 million pieces of debris larger than 1 millimeter exist in Earth's orbit. This statistic highlights the urgency of addressing the growing space debris problem. As launches become more frequent, international cooperation, improved spacecraft design, and responsible debris mitigation will be essential to ensuring that Earth's orbit remains usable for future generations.
